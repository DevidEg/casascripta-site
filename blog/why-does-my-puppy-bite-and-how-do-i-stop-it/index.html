Why does my puppy bite, and how do I stop it?

By Devid Egli, Casa Scripta

Last updated 24 November 2026

How this was written: Researched from the sources listed below and drafted with AI assistance, then edited by Devid. Not reviewed by a vet.

You've had the puppy home for a few days, and those sharp teeth keep landing on your hands, ankles and clothes. So you wonder: why does my puppy bite, and how do I stop it? Puppy biting is normal. Puppies explore the world with their mouths, and most grow out of it (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting). Here's how to tell play mouthing from a warning, and what to do next, drawn from official guidance behind our guide Home with a Puppy.

Not veterinary advice. This is general information only. Talk to your vet or a qualified behavior professional about your puppy. In an emergency, contact your nearest emergency vet.

Why does my puppy bite?

Mouthing is how puppies explore and play (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting). Teeth also get sore. Baby teeth start to fall out at around 12 weeks. Gums can be sore, and chewing can soothe them. Adult teeth are usually all in by about 7 months (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting).

Biting isn't your puppy trying to be the boss. A puppy who mouths in play is relaxed. A dog who feels threatened looks different: a stiff body, pulled-back lips and bared teeth. That's a sign to give space and get help (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting).

Tiredness is another big reason. Puppies need a lot of sleep, about 18 to 20 hours a day. An overtired puppy often gets the zoomies, wants to play, and mouths more. It's easy to think they need more exercise, when what they really need is a nap (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting).

There's no magic age when mouthing stops. Many dogs mouth a lot less once teething ends, around 7 months (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting). So this is a skill you practice, not a switch you flip. In the first week, biting may peak when your puppy is tired, so protect naps. Around weeks 5 to 8, baby teeth start to fall out and chewing may go up, so offer more chews. Week 12 is a check-in, not a finish line (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting).

How to stop puppy biting

Give teeth a better target, and pause the fun when teeth touch skin (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting). The book's steps, drawn from Dogs Trust, are:

  1. Spot the build-up. Bouncing, pouncing and batting with paws mean play is coming. Offer a toy before teeth land on you.
  2. Keep toys within reach, in each room and in your pocket.
  3. Teeth on skin? Stop and go still. Drop the toy or hide it behind your back.
  4. Still mouthing? Turn away and fold your arms. Stand up if you're on the floor.
  5. Still going? Step away for a moment. Use a door or baby gate if they follow and nip your feet.
  6. Calm again? Bring the fun back. Restart the game, scatter some treats, or give a gentle stroke.
  7. Don't snatch hands or clothes away. Fast moves make it a game. Stay still, and redirect when there's a pause.

(Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting)

If your puppy grabs clothes, don't pull away. Take off the robe or coat if you can. If they bite kids' feet, the child should stand still, wear boots if that helps, and an adult should redirect to a toy. Thick clothes or rubber boots can help kids feel safe while they redirect the puppy to a toy (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting). Evening zoomies and biting are often a nap problem: offer a rest first.

In the book, you move on when most bites stop by step 4, for 3 days in a row. If biting gets worse, check that your puppy is getting enough naps. Those timing lines are plan choices, not results from a study.

Set the house up so the steps can work

Pick 3 or 4 toys and rotate them every couple of days, so they seem new. Replace damaged toys, so pieces can't be swallowed (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting). When teething starts, around 12 weeks, offer safe chews as well, and watch for signs of pain (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting).

Don't wrestle or let your puppy bite your hands or feet, even in fun. That teaches them it's okay (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting). Everyone who handles your puppy needs to do the same things. Consistency helps puppies learn. Dogs Trust suggests writing your house rules down and sticking them on the fridge, and asking visitors to follow them too (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting).

Teach kids to give the puppy space when it's eating, sleeping, toileting or walking away. Kids should never chase, corner or roughly handle a puppy (RSPCA, puppy care). Supervise young children with dogs every time, and never leave a baby or small child alone with a dog (AVMA, dog bite prevention).

Use rewards, not harsh methods

The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior (AVSAB) says reward-based training should be used for teaching skills and for problem behavior. It says there's no evidence that harsh methods are needed, and that methods that use force, pain or fear should not be used. Surveys link harsh methods with later behavior problems, such as aggression and anxiety. They can't prove cause, but they show harsh methods don't fix those problems. A trainer who uses rewards avoids choke chains, prong collars, shock collars, shouting and "alpha" rolls (AVSAB, humane dog training).

When to call your vet

Call your vet soon, not as an emergency, if mouthing feels intense, is hard to stop, or worries you; if your puppy growls, snarls, shows teeth, or goes stiff when handled; or if you see signs of pain or teething trouble (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting). If your puppy growls or stiffens when handled, stop, give space, and call your vet.

If a person needs paramedics after a dog bite, call 911 in the US (AVMA, dog bite prevention). In any emergency for your puppy, call your vet or the nearest emergency vet clinic. If your gut still says something is wrong, trust it and call.

FAQ

Is my puppy trying to be the boss? No. Biting isn't about dominance. It's often play, teething or a sign of worry (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting).

Will more exercise stop the biting? Not if the problem is tiredness. Overtired puppies often mouth more. They may need a nap, not more exercise. Puppies need about 18 to 20 hours of sleep a day (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting).

When will the biting stop? There's no magic age. Many dogs mouth a lot less once teething ends, around 7 months, but keep redirecting until it eases (Dogs Trust, mouthing, nipping and puppy biting).
