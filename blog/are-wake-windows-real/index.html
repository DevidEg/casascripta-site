Are wake windows real? What the evidence says, and what to watch instead

By Devid Egli, Casa Scripta

Last updated 21 November 2026

How this was written: researched from the sources listed below and drafted with AI assistance and reviewed by Devid. Not reviewed by a doctor or sleep specialist.

You've seen the charts. "Wake windows 4 month old: 1 hour 45 minutes." Miss the window by ten minutes and, the app says, your baby will be overtired and the whole day falls apart. So you watch the clock, and you feel like you're failing every time a nap goes wrong. Are wake windows real? The honest answer is "sort of." The idea behind them is sensible. The exact numbers are not tested rules. Here's what the evidence says, what the rough guides suggest, and what to watch instead.

Not medical advice. This is general information only. Talk to your pediatrician, doctor or child health nurse about your baby. In an emergency, call 911 (US and Canada), 999 (UK) or 000 (Australia).

Are wake windows real? The short answer

A "wake window" is the time your baby is awake between sleeps. That part is real: every baby has awake time, and it gets longer as they grow.

What isn't real is the idea that there's one exact number for every age. When I researched our book, I found no peer-reviewed study of wake windows at all. The numbers you see online are expert advice and rough guides, not tested rules. They're a starting point, not a test your baby can pass or fail.

Babies vary a lot. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine says sleep need is shaped by genes, behavior, health and environment (Paruthi et al. 2016). A chart can't know your baby.

Where the numbers come from

Some trusted health services do give rough awake times. Tresillian, a child and family health service in Australia, uses the term itself. Its sample routines list a "3 - 4 month old wake window" of "2 hours or so," and a "5 - 6 month old wake window" of "2 - 3 hours" (Tresillian).

Notice how loose those are. "Or so." A range of a whole hour. That's very different from an app that gives a number to the minute.

Do wake windows matter?

They matter as a rough clue, not as a rule. Here's how I'd think about it.

What helps: knowing roughly when your baby might get tired, so you can start watching for signs. Tresillian says responding early to tired signs "prevents your baby becoming distressed and makes it easier" for them to sleep (Tresillian). Raising Children Network suggests putting your baby down for sleep "when they show tired signs" (Raising Children Network). Both point at your baby, not the clock.

What may not help: following strict schedules to the minute. In a UK survey of 354 mothers, those who read baby-care books that promote strict routines were less likely to feed responsively, cuddle their baby to sleep or respond promptly to cries (Harries & Brown 2019). That study shows a link, not cause and effect, but it's a good reason to hold the clock loosely.

A note on "keep them up longer." Some charts push you to stretch awake time so your baby sleeps longer at night. In a large US poll of parents of children from newborns to 10 years, a bedtime after 9 pm was linked with taking longer to fall asleep and shorter total sleep, not more sleep (Mindell et al. 2009). That's about bedtime, not naps, but it doesn't support pushing a tired baby to stay awake.

Wake windows for a 4 month old

If you want a number for wake windows at 4 months, here's what one trusted source says:

  • Awake time: about 2 hours at 3 to 4 months (Tresillian).
  • Day sleeps: about 3 naps, 3 to 4 hours in total (Tresillian).
  • Short naps are normal. At 4 to 5 months, a sleep cycle is around 30 to 50 minutes, and Tresillian says it's "very common for babies to wake up after 30-40 minutes sleep at this age" (Tresillian).

So if your 4-month-old wakes after 35 minutes, they didn't miss their window. That's a normal nap at this age. Night waking is normal, too. Babies under six months old often wake regularly at night, and the Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne says "This is normal" (RCH). Wake windows for a 6 month old

For wake windows at 6 months, the rough guides stretch out:

  • Awake time: about 2 to 3 hours at 5 to 6 months (Tresillian).
  • Day sleeps: 2 to 3 naps, 3 to 4 hours in total (Tresillian).
  • After 6 months: most babies aged 6 to 12 months still have 1 or 2 daytime naps, usually 30 minutes to 2 hours each (Raising Children Network).

The range at 6 months is a full hour wide, and that's the point. Some babies are happy awake for 3 hours. Others are rubbing their eyes after 2. Both are normal.

Watch your baby, not the clock

Tired signs are your best clue. Tresillian lists these: grimacing, yawning, grizzling, frowning, sucking, staring, snuggling in, jerky movements, becoming over active, clenching fists, rubbing eyes, fussiness or crying (Tresillian).

Here's a simple way to use a wake window without being ruled by it:

  1. Note when your baby wakes up.
  2. Use the rough guide as an alarm, not a deadline. As your baby nears the low end of the range, start watching.
  3. Act on the first tired sign you see, even if it's earlier than the chart says.
  4. If there are no tired signs, you don't have to force a nap. Keep things calm and keep watching.

Your baby is the expert on your baby. Tresillian also says that if your baby is happy during wake periods in the day, "they have had enough sleep" (Tresillian).

How much sleep is enough?

Total sleep is a better guide than any single window. At 3 to 6 months, babies usually sleep 12 to 15 hours in every 24 (Raising Children Network). Tresillian says most babies this age sleep 14 or 15 hours across day and night (Tresillian). For babies 4 to 12 months, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends 12 to 16 hours per 24 hours, including naps, and the AAP has endorsed this (Paruthi et al. 2016).

Those are wide ranges. A baby at the low end can still be doing fine.

A 3-day sleep diary beats any chart

The best way to find your baby's real pattern is to write it down. In Gentler Nights, this is Worksheet 1: 3-Day Sleep Diary. For 3 days, jot down:

  • when your baby wakes and falls asleep
  • where they slept and how they fell asleep
  • any tired signs you noticed, and when

After 3 days, look back. You'll often see your baby's own rhythm. Maybe they're usually yawning after about 2 hours and 15 minutes. That's your baby's wake window. It's the only one that counts. The diary is also a calm "before" picture, so you can see real progress later.

Safe sleep, every sleep

Whatever schedule you follow, safe sleep doesn't change. The AAP recommends putting your baby on their back, on a firm, flat (not inclined) surface, sharing your room but not your bed, with no soft bedding, and not letting them get too hot (AAP 2022). When to talk to your doctor

A rough wake window is fine to try on your own. Talk to your pediatrician, doctor or child health nurse if:

  • your baby snores, seems to pause when breathing, or has trouble breathing (RCH)
  • you're worried your baby is sleeping too little or too much (Paruthi et al. 2016)
  • you're struggling to cope. The RCH says to speak with your doctor or nurse if you have concerns about how you're coping (RCH)

If your baby has trouble breathing or seems very unwell, call your emergency number.

FAQ

My baby fights naps even at the "right" time. Is something wrong? Not usually. Rough guides are only examples, and babies vary. Try watching for tired signs instead, and see what your diary shows.

My 4-month-old only naps for 30 to 40 minutes. Did I get the window wrong? Probably not. Tresillian says naps of 30 to 40 minutes are very common at this age (Tresillian).

Should I wake my baby to stick to the schedule? None of the sources I used say you need to. If you're worried about how much your baby sleeps, ask your doctor or child health nurse.

Does a bedtime routine help more than wake windows? A short, calm routine is one of the gentlest things you can add. See my bedtime routine guide for 4 to 6 month olds.
