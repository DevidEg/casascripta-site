What to do when your toddler won't stay in bed

By Devid Egli, Casa Scripta

Last updated 24 November 2026

How this was written: Researched from the sources listed below and drafted with AI assistance, then edited by Devid. Not reviewed by a doctor or sleep specialist.

Lights out, then the door opens again. One more book. A drink. The toilet. Then they're in the hall. If you're wondering what to do when your toddler won't stay in bed, you're in a very common stretch of family life. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says many parents find their toddler's bedtime the hardest part of the day (AAP Bedtime). Here's a calm way through it, drawn from official guidance and the research behind our guide Tucked In.

Not medical advice. This is general information only. Talk to your pediatrician, doctor or child health nurse about your child. In an emergency, call 911 (US and Canada), 999 (UK) or 000 (Australia).

Why your toddler won't stay in bed

Sleep trouble is common at this age. Toddlers are growing, learning new skills, testing limits and wanting comfort, all at once, and sleep problems are one of the most common reasons parents ask for help (Raising Children Network, toddler sleep). A child's main goal at bedtime is often to get you to appear. If you appear for any reason, they'll expect you each time they call (AAP Bedtime). If they take more than 30 minutes to fall asleep, bedtime may be too early: start at the time they can actually fall asleep, then move it earlier later (Raising Children Network, calling out). Once they can get out of bed alone, the whole room has to be safe to explore at night (Raising Children Network, cot to bed).

Start with the same short routine

Change one thing first: how the last half hour of the day runs. You don't need new stay-in-bed rules tonight. You need a routine that looks the same tonight, tomorrow and the night after.

In one trial with 405 mothers of children aged 7 to 36 months, families who used a set nightly routine for two weeks saw their children fall asleep faster and wake less at night, and the mothers' mood improved too (Mindell 2009). A survey of over 10,000 children up to age 5 found that the more nights a week families used a routine, the better children slept. That's a link, not proof (Mindell 2015).

Keep it to four or five calm steps, in the same order. A common set is a snack or milk, a bath or wash, pajamas, teeth, books, then a cuddle and goodnight (AAP Brush, Book, Bed; Raising Children Network, toddler sleep). Finish milk at least 30 minutes before bed, because milk in bed can cause tooth decay, and make brushing the last thing that touches the teeth (Raising Children Network, toddler sleep; AAP Brush, Book, Bed). Turn screens off 60 minutes before bed, or start with at least 30 minutes, the lowest gap the NHS gives, and work up (AAP sleep hours; NHS, sleep and young children). Start the routine about 30 minutes before your toddler usually falls asleep, using the time they really fall asleep now, and decide the limit ahead of time, such as one story, then tuck in (NHS, sleep and young children). A safe comfort toy or blanket is fine at this age, and so is a night-light if it helps (AAP Bedtime; RCH sleep). Say the same goodnight words every night, such as "Night night, I love you, see you in the morning" (NHS Bedfordshire).

After three nights, the routine should take about the same time each night and feel less like a battle. If it takes more than 45 minutes, cut one step or one book. That 45-minute line is a plan choice, not a result from a study.

One calm reply, as many times as it takes

Once the routine is familiar, use the same brief, boring response every time they call or get out of bed (AAP Big Kid Beds; NHS, sleep and young children).

A team set up by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine reviewed 52 studies of behavioral sleep plans for young children. In 94% of the studies the plans worked, and over 80% of the children improved in ways that still held 3 to 6 months later. The review found support for set routines, for moving bedtime earlier step by step, and for checking in at set times. It also found strong support for leaving a child to cry without checks. Tucked In doesn't use that. Those studies tested many different plans, not this exact one, and results vary (Mindell 2006).

The book's calm reply, built on Raising Children Network and NHS advice, looks like this:

  1. After goodnight, say what happens next, using words like these from Raising Children Network: "It's time to rest quietly in bed. I'll come back and check on you once you're quiet." Then walk out (Raising Children Network, calling out).
  2. If they call out, answer from the door, once: "I'm here. It's sleep time." If they've already had their drink, toilet and stories, it's OK not to go in with another (Raising Children Network, calling out).
  3. If they get out of bed, say, "It's time to sleep. Please stay in your bed." Walk them back gently, with no talking and no scolding, as many times as it takes. The aim is to praise staying in bed, not getting out (Raising Children Network, calling out; NHS, sleep and young children; AAP Big Kid Beds).
  4. When they're quiet, keep your promise and praise them briefly: "Well done for staying in bed. You're doing a good job of resting." In the book, the first check comes after about 5 quiet minutes. That timing is a plan choice (Raising Children Network, calling out).
  5. Night wakings get the same reply: lights off, few words, and as boring as you can be. If your child is unwell or scared, comfort them first (NHS, sleep and young children).
  6. In the morning, praise any time they stayed in bed, and don't go over a rough night (Raising Children Network, calling out).

More trips out in the first few nights is common. When a toddler first moves to a big bed, twenty "farewell appearances" in one evening isn't unusual (AAP Big Kid Beds). If it isn't settling, check that every adult is giving the same reply, and that bedtime isn't earlier than the time your child can actually fall asleep. Routines work best when every adult who does bedtime uses them the same way (Raising Children Network, calling out).

The daytime pieces that help bedtime hold

For a few days, keep the new routine and change nothing else at night. Use the same wake-up time every day, weekends too, with curtains open and bright light in the morning, and keep screens out of the bedroom (AAP sleep hours). End the nap by 3 p.m., and keep it to 2 hours or less (NHS Bedfordshire; Raising Children Network, toddler sleep). Children aged 1 to 2 years need 11 to 14 hours of sleep in 24 hours, naps included; children aged 3 to 5 years need 10 to 13 hours (AASM 2016; WHO 2019). Putting them to bed later so they'll sleep in doesn't tend to work. They may still wake early and be grumpy from too little sleep (Raising Children Network, toddler sleep). Once your child is falling asleep well at their starting bedtime, you can move bedtime earlier by about 15 minutes every few days, for example every 3 nights (Raising Children Network, bedtime fading; Mindell 2006).

Two extras if they keep coming out

A bedtime pass, for age 3 and over. Give your child a card at bedtime, good for one drink, one extra hug, or one trip out. After they use it, the usual calm reply applies for the rest of the night. If they don't use it, Raising Children Network suggests they can use it the next day or trade it for a special activity you agreed on beforehand (Raising Children Network, calling out). In a small trial of 19 children aged 3 to 6, children with the pass left their rooms and called out much less than children without it, and the gains held 3 months later. That's a small trial, so treat it as limited evidence (Moore 2007).

A safety gate, only in a room you've already made safe. Raising Children Network suggests saying, "If you don't stay in bed, I'll close the gate, and I'll open it again when you're staying in bed. Would you like one more chance?" Then close the gate if they don't stay in bed (Raising Children Network, calling out). First walk the room at your child's eye level: furniture bracketed to the wall, window locks, cords out of reach, medicines and small objects out of the room, and a gate at the top of the stairs (Raising Children Network, cot to bed; AAP Big Kid Beds). If these steps don't feel right for your child, it's OK to go back to what you did before and try again later (Raising Children Network, calling out).

When to talk to your doctor

If your toddler snores loudly, pauses or gasps in sleep, or has trouble breathing while asleep, talk to a doctor before you start any sleep plan. About 15 to 20 out of 100 children snore, but only about 2 or 3 out of 100 have obstructive sleep apnea, where the airway gets blocked during sleep. Snoring with pauses, gasps or daytime sleepiness needs a doctor's check (RCH sleep; RCH OSA).

See your child's doctor before you start a new bedtime plan if:

  • There is loud snoring, pauses in breathing, choking, gasping or snorting in sleep, restless or sweaty sleep, morning headaches or tiredness, or new trouble with attention or behavior in the day (RCH OSA).
  • Sleep got worse along with pain, fever or illness. Pause until your child is well (RCH sleep).
  • Your child has lost skills they once had, or isn't meeting milestones (CDC milestones).
  • The trouble started after a big change, loss or scary event, or your child seems very afraid of the night or of being apart from you (Raising Children Network, calling out).
  • The problem has lasted more than a month, even with good sleep habits (Raising Children Network, persistent sleep problems).
  • You're thinking about melatonin or a sleep medicine. Talk to your child's doctor first. In the US, the amount of melatonin in supplements can range from less than half to more than four times what the label says (AASM melatonin advisory).

Check with your child's doctor first if your child has a medical condition or a developmental difference that affects sleep. If your gut still says something is wrong, trust it and ask.

FAQ

How many times should I walk them back? As many times as it takes, briefly and without scolding. A run of extra trips in the first few nights is common (Raising Children Network, calling out; AAP Big Kid Beds).

Will a later bedtime make them stay in bed? For toddlers, putting them to bed later so they'll sleep in doesn't tend to work. They may still wake early and be grumpy from too little sleep. If they take more than 30 minutes to fall asleep, the bedtime may actually be too early (Raising Children Network, toddler sleep; Raising Children Network, calling out).

What if they share a room with a sibling? Raising Children Network suggests delaying the older child's bedtime by about half an hour until your toddler is asleep (Raising Children Network, toddler sleep).
