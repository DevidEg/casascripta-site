What to do when your toddler won't eat

By Devid Egli, Casa Scripta

Last updated 24 November 2026

How this was written: Researched from the sources listed below and drafted with AI assistance, then edited by Devid. Not reviewed by a doctor or dietitian.

Dinner is on the table, and your toddler won't eat. Maybe they loved this food last week. Maybe the meal turns into pleading, bargaining or a second dinner later. If you're wondering what to do when your toddler won't eat, start with how the meal feels, not with a new recipe. Here's a method drawn from official guidance and the research behind our guide Peace at the Table.

Not medical advice. This is general information only. Talk to your pediatrician, doctor, child health nurse or dietitian about your child. In an emergency, call 911 (US and Canada), 999 (UK) or 000 (Australia).

Why your toddler won't eat

Picky eating is often a normal part of toddler life. In a US survey of more than 3,000 young children, the share that caregivers called picky rose from 19 out of 100 at 4 months to 50 out of 100 at 24 months (Carruth 2004). After the fast growth of the first year, a toddler's growth slows down, and so does their appetite. A favorite food one day may hit the floor the next (AAP, picky eaters). Choosing what to eat, and what not to eat, is part of growing up (Raising Children Network, fussy eating).

Appetite also goes up and down. Between ages 1 and 6, it's common for a child to be very hungry one day and picky the next (Raising Children Network, fussy eating). Look at what they eat over a week, not a day (NHS, fussy eaters; Raising Children Network, family meals). The NHS says that if your child is active, gaining weight and seems well, they're getting enough to eat (NHS, fussy eaters). In a study that followed 120 children from age 2 to 11, between 13 and 22 out of 100 were picky at any one age, and picky eating had no clear effect on growth. That was a small study, so treat it as limited evidence (Mascola 2010).

Drinks and snacks can fill a small tummy before a meal. A child who fills up on drinks or grazes is less likely to try food at the table (Raising Children Network, fussy eating). And pressure often backfires. In one study, preschoolers ate more when they weren't pressured to eat, and they made far fewer negative comments about the food (Galloway 2006). The AAP says pressuring or punishing children for not eating can make them dislike foods they might otherwise like (AAP, picky eaters).

Tonight: one calm meal, no new foods

Tonight you change one thing: how dinner feels. You don't need to get a vegetable into your child. You just need one meal where nobody pushes, pleads or bargains.

  1. Put one or two foods your child usually eats on the table with the family meal. A safe food is one they eat most of the time, such as bread, rice or fruit (Ellyn Satter Institute, feeding at every age).
  2. Serve small amounts, and let your child serve themselves from the middle of the table if they can (NHS, fussy eaters; Raising Children Network, fussy eating).
  3. Turn off the TV and put phones away (AAP, picky eaters; Raising Children Network, fussy eating).
  4. Sit and eat together. Children learn to eat new foods by copying you. Chat about other things (NHS, fussy eaters).
  5. Say nothing about how much they eat. Let them eat in any order, and as much or as little as they want (Ellyn Satter Institute, feeding at every age).
  6. End after about 20 minutes. If food isn't eaten, take it away without fuss or comment (Raising Children Network, fussy eating; NHS, fussy eaters).
  7. Don't cook a second meal if they refuse what you served. The next snack comes at the usual time (AAP, picky eaters).

After three meals, ask yourself: did the table feel calmer? That's the only goal for now. If meals run past 30 minutes, shorten them. Those "3 meals" and "30 minutes" lines are plan choices, not results from a study.

Useful words, built on the same split of jobs: "You don't have to eat it." "You can have more of anything on the table." "Lunch is finished. Snack is at 3." Words to skip: "Just one more bite." "No dessert unless…" "Good job finishing!"

The jobs at the table

The method rests on a clear split of jobs, from Ellyn Satter's Division of Responsibility: you choose what, when and where; your child chooses whether and how much (Ellyn Satter Institute, Division of Responsibility).

For one or two weeks, don't add new foods. Build the routine that makes later tasting possible:

  • Three meals a day, with sit-down snacks at set times, and water only between times (Ellyn Satter Institute, feeding at every age). The NHS says 2 healthy snacks a day is plenty (NHS, fussy eaters). Don't leave meals until your child is too hungry or tired to eat (NHS, fussy eaters).
  • Serve one family meal, including one or two foods your child usually eats. Don't cook a separate meal if they refuse (Ellyn Satter Institute, feeding at every age; AAP, picky eaters).
  • Let them leave when they're done. Once they get down, the meal is over for them (Ellyn Satter Institute, feeding at every age).
  • Get everyone on board. Grandparents and sitters need the same rules: no pressure and no food bribes (AAP, picky eaters; NHS, fussy eaters).

If your child refuses dinner and asks for a snack later, the snack comes at the usual time, with water until then (Ellyn Satter Institute, feeding at every age). If they eat only the safe food, that's allowed. Keep serving the family meal around it. If they refuse food to see how you'll react, staying calm makes it less interesting (Raising Children Network, fussy eating). A 5-minute warning before meals, and hand-washing as a ritual, can help them settle before they sit down.

Drinks, snacks and bribes

The AAP suggests water and plain milk as the main drinks: about 2 cups (16 oz) of milk a day from 12 to 24 months, and 2 to 3 cups (16 to 24 oz) a day from 2 to 5 years. More than 32 oz of milk a day may make it harder to absorb iron (AAP, recommended drinks; AAP, your 2-year-old). If you give 100% juice, the AAP says no more than 4 oz a day at ages 2 to 3, and 4 to 6 oz at ages 4 to 5. Whole fruit is better than juice (AAP, recommended drinks). Bring milk and juice into meal and snack times, with water only in between.

Bribes with treats are a common trap. The AAP says they make the "prize" food more exciting and the other food a chore (AAP, picky eaters). The NHS says not to use food as a reward, and suggests a trip to the park instead (NHS, fussy eaters).

Offer again, without pressure

Once meals are calmer, you can start offering one new food, in a tiny amount, without turning dinner into a tasting lesson every night. It can take 10 or more tastes before a child accepts a new food (AAP, picky eaters; Raising Children Network, fussy eating). In one survey, caregivers often gave up after 3 to 5 tries, while the authors suggest 8 to 15 (Carruth 2004).

In a trial with 156 families of children aged 2 to 6, parents gave their child a small taste of a disliked vegetable every day for 14 days. Those children liked and ate the vegetable more than children in the other groups (Wardle 2003). A review found the same pattern: children like what they know, and repeated tastes build liking (Cooke 2007).

Keep the offer light. A pea-sized piece is enough, cut safely for age. Say once, "Here's a little taste if you'd like it." Then talk about something else. If they push it away, take it away without a word (NHS, fussy eaters; Raising Children Network, choking). Count looking, touching, smelling and licking as steps forward. Some children need many more tries than a few weeks allows. In one family-feeding approach, children learn to eat the family's food "over a long time for some" (Ellyn Satter Institute, Division of Responsibility).

Children under 4 are at higher risk of choking, so cut and cook food safely, have your child sit down to eat, and stay with them (Raising Children Network, choking; AAP, choking prevention).

When to talk to your doctor

Most picky eating is part of normal development (AAP, picky eaters). Some children need a doctor first. If your child is losing weight or not growing as expected, eats very few foods, often gags, chokes or vomits with food, or is very upset at meals, see a doctor before you start a new feeding plan (RCH nutrition; Nationwide Children's, pediatric feeding disorder; Children's Colorado, feeding and swallowing).

See your child's doctor if:

  • Your child has lost weight, or isn't gaining weight or growing as expected (RCH nutrition; Nationwide Children's, pediatric feeding disorder).
  • They eat fewer than 5 foods as a toddler, or fewer than 15 by preschool age, or their list of foods keeps getting shorter (Nationwide Children's, pediatric feeding disorder).
  • Whole food groups have been left out for more than a few weeks (Raising Children Network, fussy eating; RCH feeding).
  • They often choke, gag, cough or vomit while eating or right after, or have trouble chewing or swallowing (Children's Colorado, feeding and swallowing).
  • Meals often last more than 30 minutes, even with foods they like, or they are very upset when new foods appear (RCH feeding).
  • You notice a rash, tingling in or around the mouth, or face swelling after eating. These can be signs of a food allergy (RCH nutrition).

If your gut still says something is wrong, trust it and ask. Check with your child's care team first if your child has a medical condition or a known food allergy. In an emergency, such as choking that won't clear or signs of a serious allergic reaction (swelling of the lips, mouth, tongue or throat; hard or noisy breathing; a child who is pale, floppy or not responding as usual), call 911 in the US or Canada, 999 in the UK, or 000 in Australia.

FAQ

They only ate the safe food. Did the meal fail? No. A safe food on the table is part of the plan. Keep serving the family meal around it, and let your child decide whether and how much (Ellyn Satter Institute, feeding at every age).

Should I bribe them with dessert? The AAP says not to bribe with treats, because that makes the prize food more exciting and the other food a chore (AAP, picky eaters). If you serve dessert, one option is a small portion with the meal, so it isn't a prize, and the child can eat it in any order (Ellyn Satter Institute, feeding at every age). Pick one way and stick with it.

When do children stop being picky eaters? There isn't one age. In a study that followed children from age 2 to 11, between 13 and 22 out of 100 were picky at any one age (Mascola 2010). Some children need many more tries than a few weeks allows. Judge progress over a week, not a meal, and see a doctor if foods keep dropping off the list or growth is a worry.
